01Miracles

Miracles and the gift of miracles

THE CRITICISM

“the view that miracles “ceased” long ago and there is no hope for signs and wonders since we have the Bible”

R. T. Kendall

Citation & context +

RT’s New Year Letter, “Two things will change,” second change New Year’s Letter 2013, RT Kendall Ministries

Generalization about belief

This parenthetical defines cessationism in Kendall’s prediction of a coming spiritual awakening. Schreiner’s explicit affirmation of present miracles qualifies that broad description; it does not establish what every cessationist believes.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“I believe God still heals and does miraculous things today”

Thomas R. Schreiner

Citation & context +

Why I Am a Cessationist, “Miracles and Healings” Source: The Gospel Coalition, January 22, 2014

Miracles and the gift of miracles

Schreiner affirms present-day miracles while questioning whether individuals possess the New Testament gifts of healing and miracles. A claim about the continuation of a gift is different from a claim that God never acts miraculously.

Read in context. He doubts that these gifts are present in churches today, calls for verification of healing claims, and allows for extraordinary signs in missionary settings. These qualifications describe his position; they should not be assigned to every cessationist.

More cessationist voices · 1 +
“Now may I quickly say, God is still doing miracles today?”

John MacArthur · The Gifts of the Body, Part 3, discussion following the purpose of authenticating miracles Grace to You sermon transcript, February 7, 1971; sermon 1313

MacArthur immediately distinguishes these miracles from signs authenticating new revelation. He attributes present miracles to God’s sovereign purpose and answers to faith and prayer, apart from a person’s miraculous gift. This affirmation does not endorse contemporary claims to possess the apostolic sign gifts.

Confessional witness · 2 +
“yet is free to work without, above, and against them, at his pleasure.”
Westminster Confession of Faith 5.3

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

“Them” refers to ordinary means. This affirms extraordinary providence; it neither promises particular miracles nor establishes continuing gifts of miracle-working.

“yet is free to work without, above, and against them at His pleasure.”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 5.3

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

The Baptist confession likewise affirms God’s freedom beyond ordinary means. This supports openness to extraordinary divine action without deciding whether particular miraculous gifts continue.

02Healing & prayer

Praying for what God can do

THE CRITICISM

“You will not likely convince a cessationist to believe that the living God heals supernaturally today.”

R. T. Kendall

Citation & context +

Theology and worship, paragraph beginning “You will not likely convince” RT Kendall Ministries, online article; no publication date displayed

Generalization about belief

Kendall criticizes both charismatic excesses and conservative evangelical cessationism, while describing cessationists as good people rather than heretics. This sentence concerns belief in supernatural healing, not a claim that cessationists never pray for the sick.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“Still, I pray for the healing of the sick and believe God can do so miraculously.”

Thomas R. Schreiner

Citation & context +

It All Depends Upon Prophecy: A Brief Case for Nuanced Cessationism, opening discussion Source: Themelios 44.1 (2019), pp. 29–35; excerpt on p. 29

Praying for what God can do

Schreiner explicitly prays for miraculous healing. His stated belief and practice provide a direct counterexample to Kendall’s broad expectation about cessationists’ disbelief in supernatural healing.

Read in context. He regards miracles as relatively rare and distinguishes them from a gift regularly exercised by believers. He calls his position nuanced cessationism and allows for exceptional missionary situations. This is neither a promise that every prayer will result in healing nor a survey of all cessationist views.

More cessationist voices · 1 +
“the sovereign power of God to heal the sick, particularly in response to prayer, is a reality experienced”

OPC Committee on the Baptism and Gifts of the Holy Spirit · Report of the Committee on the Baptism and Gifts of the Holy Spirit, V. Gifts of Healing in the Church Report presented to the Forty-fifth General Assembly of the Orthodox Presbyterian Church, 1978

The report distinguishes apostolic healing gifts from God’s continuing power to heal in response to prayer. It goes on to call this an expectation throughout church history. The committee was chaired by Richard B. Gaffin, Jr.; the assembly received the report for study. It is a committee report, not a constitutional confession or an individual statement by Gaffin.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“Prayer is to be made for things lawful;”
Westminster Confession of Faith 21.4

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

This general rule permits lawful petitions, including healing. It does not specifically command miraculous healing or guarantee that the sick will recover.

03Spiritual gifts

Some gifts cease; the Spirit keeps giving

THE CRITICISM

“In a word: the gifts of the Spirit are unavailable today”

RT Kendall Ministries (published teaching notes)

Citation & context +

The Gifts and the Fruit of the Holy Spirit, Introduction B.3.b.(2), p. 2 Friday Night School of Theology notes hosted by RT Kendall Ministries; undated PDF

Scope needs clarification

These unbylined notes, published on Kendall’s ministry website, discuss cessationism alongside signs and wonders and distinguish gifts from fruit. The surrounding focus on miraculous gifts matters: this wording must not be inflated into a denial that cessationists affirm teaching, service, or every other continuing gift.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“It is the same Spirit who gives both temporary and continuing gifts to the church.”

Sinclair B. Ferguson

Citation & context +

The Holy Spirit’s Ministry, paragraph beginning “What does this mean?” Source: Ligonier Ministries, August 6, 2018; excerpt from The Holy Spirit

Some gifts cease; the Spirit keeps giving

Ferguson explicitly distinguishes temporary gifts from gifts that continue. His argument concerns particular gifts, rather than the withdrawal of every spiritual gift from the church.

Read in context. Ferguson identifies miracle-working, revelatory prophecy, and tongues as temporary, while affirming continuing gifts and illumination. Kendall’s surrounding focus is miraculous gifts, so the dispute should not be inflated into a claim that he denies cessationists affirm any gifts at all. The precise scope of “gifts” matters.

More cessationist voices · 1 +
“We said there are two categories of gifts: permanent and temporary.”

John MacArthur · The Gifts of the Body, Part 3, opening distinction between permanent and temporary gifts Grace to You sermon transcript, February 7, 1971; sermon 1313

MacArthur explains that permanent gifts continue to build up the church, while temporary gifts confirmed the apostles’ and prophets’ message. This is an explicit distinction within spiritual gifts, not a rejection of all gifts. It does not resolve the disagreement over which gifts continue.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“they have communion in each other’s gifts and graces,”
Westminster Confession of Faith 26.1

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

Believers share gifts for mutual good. This affirms continuing gifted service without identifying whether prophecy, tongues, or healing belong among those gifts.

04The Holy Spirit

The Spirit’s present power

THE CRITICISM

“without any question quenched the Holy Spirit Himself in the process.”

R. T. Kendall

Citation & context +

Holy Fire, paragraph beginning “There are two chapters” RT Kendall Ministries, article introducing the book Holy Fire; no publication date displayed

Criticism of consequences

Kendall argues that making cessationism a dogma quenches the Spirit, while affirming cessationists’ Christian character and commitment to Scripture. This is a criticism of the position’s effects, not a statement that cessationists consciously deny all the Spirit’s activity.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“We do not assert that God's Spirit is no longer actively working in dynamic and dramatic ways. We earnestly believe that he is.”

Richard B. Gaffin, Jr.

Citation & context +

What about Prophecy and Tongues Today?, second paragraph Source: Orthodox Presbyterian Church, online article

The Spirit’s present power

Gaffin’s affirmation establishes that he believes in the Spirit’s active power today. It answers a blanket description of cessationism as denying the Spirit’s work, but it does not by itself refute Kendall’s criticism that cessationism hinders particular activities of the Spirit.

Read in context. Gaffin points to conversion and continuing gifts while denying foundational apostles and revelatory prophecy. Kendall argues that the disputed gifts remain available. Deciding whether cessationism quenches the Spirit requires addressing that biblical disagreement; quoting an affirmation of the Spirit alone is not enough.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“the inward illumination of the Spirit of God to be necessary for the saving understanding”
Westminster Confession of Faith 1.6

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

The object of this understanding is Scripture. Continuing illumination is explicitly affirmed; this does not settle whether rejecting particular gifts quenches the Spirit.

05Christian experience

Beliefs and motives are different questions

THE CRITICISM

“I also think fear is a huge factor.”

Sam Storms

Citation & context +

Pneuma Review Interview with Sam Storms on Speaking in Tongues, answer to why Christians still adhere to cessationism Enjoying God Ministries, August 8, 2019; interview answers by Sam Storms

Claim about motives

Storms also discusses inherited teaching, concern about abusive charismatic practices, and biblical texts cessationists understand differently. He offers several explanations; he does not say every cessationist is motivated only by fear.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“the fruit of the Spirit transforming us into the likeness of the character of Christ.”

Sinclair B. Ferguson

Citation & context +

How does the modern church often misunderstand the Holy Spirit?, final answer paragraph Source: Ask Ligonier transcript, lightly edited by the publisher for readability

Beliefs and motives are different questions

Ferguson gives a positive account of the Spirit’s transforming work. That helps describe what he affirms, but it cannot prove or disprove Storms’s claim about why some Christians remain cessationists. A person’s motives require evidence beyond their doctrinal label.

Read in context. Storms names several factors rather than reducing every cessationist’s motives to fear. Ferguson’s answer here addresses Christian character, not the psychology of the debate. Read these as different kinds of statements; neither excerpt settles the prevalence of fear among cessationists.

06Prophecy & revelation

An argument about the church’s foundation

THE CRITICISM

“I believe it is impossible to make an exegetically robust argument for cessationism using the Bible alone.”

Michael Brown

Citation & context +

Let the Reformed of the Lord Say No to Cessationism, paragraph beginning “Of course, each of these concepts” Christianity Today, October 9, 2023

Interpretive disagreement

Brown makes this judgment after arguing from John 14, Acts 2, 1 Corinthians, and James 5. This is a substantive challenge to cessationist exegesis, not itself a misquotation or a claim that cessationists never attempt biblical arguments.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“The foundation for the church has been laid once-for-all”

Thomas R. Schreiner

Citation & context +

The Miraculous Gifts and the Question of Cessationism, “Redemptive History” Source: The Gospel Coalition, online theological essay

An argument about the church’s foundation

Schreiner offers an argument from the church’s apostolic and prophetic foundation, especially Ephesians 2:20. This identifies a biblical rationale that readers can evaluate. Merely citing the rationale does not establish that it is exegetically sound or answer Brown’s judgment that cessationist arguments fail.

Read in context. Schreiner connects the foundation with apostolic revelation and the completed canon. Brown disputes the case by appealing to passages he understands to promise continuing gifts. This is an interpretive disagreement, not automatically a misrepresentation. Readers need to examine both arguments in their full sources.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“those former ways of God’s revealing his will unto his people being now ceased.”
Westminster Confession of Faith 1.1

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

The context is committing revelation to writing. This explicitly affirms cessation of former revelatory ways, without enumerating every disputed gift or proving its biblical case.

OUR APPROACH

A fair hearing.
A faithful reading.

This collection exists to represent cessationist writers truthfully where their teaching is misrepresented, by letting their own words be heard.

Cessationists differ over the scope of cessation, extraordinary events, and the language used to describe the Spirit’s guidance. A quotation establishes what its named author says; it is not a statement on behalf of every cessationist.

The criticism in each entry is an exact excerpt from a named writer, with a source reference and context. Some make broad claims about cessationist belief; others challenge consequences, motives, or biblical interpretation. A criticism is not automatically a misrepresentation. Responses and context notes are editorial commentary, and identify what the paired quotation can and cannot answer.

Supporting voices add another author or a committee’s published explanation where it helps clarify the issue. Committee reports are identified separately from constitutional confessions. Modern excerpts are deliberately brief. Read both linked arguments in full, including the authors’ qualifications. These six pairings represent a starting collection, not every cessationist or continuationist tradition.
