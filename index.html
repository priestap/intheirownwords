01Sovereignty

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinism denies human responsibility.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“So we can’t be responsible for our choices because nothing that we think or do is up to us.”

William Lane Craig

Doctrine of Creation, Part 10, objection 3 to universal divine causal determinism Reasonable Faith, Defenders series 2, published lecture transcript

An argument about logical consequences

Craig argues that divine causal determination removes moral responsibility. He recognizes Reformed appeals to secondary causes but finds them inadequate. This is a philosophical criticism of Calvinism’s consistency, rather than a claim that Calvinists never verbally affirm responsibility. Calvin’s quotation establishes his affirmation; it does not by itself resolve Craig’s objection.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“Man therefore falls, divine providence so ordaining, but he falls by his own fault.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 3.23.8 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Sovereignty & responsibility

Calvin places divine ordination and human fault in the same sentence. He does not treat providence as an excuse for sin.

Read in context. This passage also affirms that the fall occurs within God’s providence. Whether that is compatible with responsibility remains a substantive question.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“The cause or guilt of this unbelief as well as of all other sins, is no wise in God, but in man himself”
Canons of Dort I.5

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The article contrasts human guilt for unbelief with faith and salvation as God’s free gift.

02Human agency

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinism makes people robots or puppets.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“So the accusation that EDD makes us all puppets, is to apply the greater truth of EDD and refuse to pretend along.”

James LeBlanc

The Puppet Analogy explained, “Applied” Soteriology 101 guest article, credited “Mindtrap James LeBlanc,” July 29, 2024

An analogy offered as a consequence of exhaustive decree

EDD means exhaustive divine decree. LeBlanc argues that appealing to human consciousness does not remove the analogy’s force. He is arguing for what he thinks follows from Calvinistic determinism, not claiming Calvinists literally believe people are wooden puppets. The guest author, rather than the site’s host, is responsible for these words.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“Man, since he was corrupted by the fall, sins not forced or unwilling, but voluntarily, by a most forward bias of the mind”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 2.3.5 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

People, not puppets

Calvin distinguishes voluntary action from external compulsion. People act from their own desires.

Read in context. The surrounding passage also teaches a necessity of sinning in fallen humanity. Voluntariness here does not mean freedom from every kind of necessity.

Confessional witness · 3 +
“this grace of regeneration does not treat men as senseless stocks and blocks, nor takes away their will and its properties, neither does violence thereto”
Canons of Dort III/IV.16

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

“Stocks and blocks” means unthinking objects. The article goes on to describe grace as healing and powerfully renewing the will.

“nor is violence offered to the will of the creature”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 3.1

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

The paragraph affirms God’s decree while denying violence to the creature’s will and upholding the liberty or contingency of second causes. It does not affirm independence from God’s decree.

“His understanding, indeed, was not taken from him, neither was he deprived of his will, and altogether changed into a stone or stock.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter IX

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The chapter affirms that fallen people retain understanding and will, while teaching that those faculties are corrupted and that regeneration is necessary for spiritual good.

03Grace

THE COMMON CLAIM

Irresistible grace forces people to believe against their will.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“They make Christians into people whom God forced against their wills to become His children.”

David Servant

Calvinism Considered, chapter 2, concluding summary of total depravity and irresistible grace Author’s online text, chapter 2

A description of conversion that Calvin explicitly rejects

“They” refers to the doctrines of total depravity and irresistible grace. Servant treats inability to choose otherwise as coercion. Calvin instead describes the will being changed so that the person comes willingly. Establishing this difference in descriptions leaves their deeper disagreement about freedom and grace to be argued from Scripture.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“a powerful impulse of the Holy Spirit, which makes men willing who formerly were unwilling and reluctant.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Commentary on John 6:44 Translation: William Pringle (1847)

Made willing by grace

Grace renews the will so that a person willingly comes to Christ. Calvin explicitly distinguishes this drawing from external force.

Read in context. Calvin does teach an effective divine initiative. The disagreement concerns this renewal of the will, rather than a profession of faith extracted from someone who still rejects Christ.

Confessional witness · 4 +
“yet so, as they come most freely, being made willing by his grace.”
Westminster Confession of Faith 10.1
“by his Holy Spirit, He also assures me of eternal life, and makes me sincerely willing and ready, henceforth, to live unto him.”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 1, Q&A 1

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The catechism describes the believer’s comfort and willing obedience as fruits of belonging to Christ.

“yet so as they come most freely, being made willing by His grace.”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 10.1

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

Effectual calling is described as renewing the will and drawing sinners to Christ so that their coming is willing.

“And the will itself is not only changed by the Spirit, but it is also endued with faculties, that, of its own accord, it may both will and do good”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter IX

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The subject is the regenerate person. The chapter describes the Spirit as enabling willing action, and then says that the regenerate act actively, not only passively.

04Sovereignty

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinism makes God guilty of sin.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“there was no rational answer against the charge of divine culpability for moral evil if Calvinism’s claims were true.”

Leighton Flowers

Your Systematic Has No Clothes, opening recollection of his time as a Calvinist Soteriology 101, August 22, 2015

An argument about moral consequences

Flowers presents this as a conclusion he drew while still affirming Calvinism. He later expressly declines to accuse sincere Calvinists of devil worship. Calvin’s distinction between God’s purpose and the sinner’s intention states the position Flowers challenges; simply quoting the distinction does not prove it resolves every objection.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“the same act at once betrays the guilt of man, and manifests the righteousness of God.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 1.18.4 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Ordination & moral guilt

Calvin distinguishes the purposes of God and human agents within a single event. He affirms divine righteousness and creaturely guilt together.

Read in context. His denial of divine guilt establishes his stated position. It does not by itself prove that his account of providence is coherent.

Confessional witness · 4 +
“neither is God the author of sin”
Westminster Confession of Faith 3.1
“nevertheless, God neither is the author of, nor can be charged with, the sins which are committed.”
Belgic Confession, Article 13

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The same article affirms God’s government of all events. This denial states the confession’s position; its coherence remains a matter for theological argument.

“yet so as thereby is God neither the author of sin nor hath fellowship with any therein”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 3.1

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

This qualification accompanies the affirmation that God decrees all that comes to pass. It states the confession’s denial of divine participation in sin without by itself resolving objections to that distinction.

“Yea, there are even in ourselves sin and corruption enough, so that there is no need that God should infuse into us either a new or greater measure of wickedness.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter VIII

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The preceding paragraph condemns making God the author of sin. The following paragraphs distinguish God’s righteous judgment and government from human wickedness.

05Sovereignty

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinism is fatalism: our actions make no difference.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Saying that God ordains the means as well as the ends does not add meaning to the meaninglessness of determinism.”

Dale W. Decker

Why I’m not a Calvinist, Even Though I Should Be: Determinism, concluding discussion of ordained means Soteriology 101 guest article by Dale W. Decker, February 10, 2020; posted by Eric Kemp

A challenge to the significance of ordained means

Decker explicitly considers the Calvinist answer that God ordains means and rejects it as insufficient. His charge is not ignorance of that answer. Calvin’s insistence on using means distinguishes his practical teaching from passive fatalism; Decker’s philosophical objection remains a further question.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“the eternal decrees of God by no means prevent us from proceeding, under his will, to provide for ourselves, and arrange all our affairs.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 1.17.4 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

The significance of means

Providence includes the means through which events happen. Planning, precautions, and ordinary care remain meaningful duties.

Read in context. Calvin immediately discusses the means of preserving life and avoiding danger. His argument does not promise that every plan will succeed.

Confessional witness · 3 +
“who watches over us with a paternal care, keeping all creatures so under his power”
Belgic Confession, Article 13

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The article presents providence as fatherly care, rather than impersonal fate. This excerpt addresses the character of providence, not by itself the question of how human means work.

“God, in his ordinary providence makes use of means”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 5.3

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

The paragraph also says God is free to work without, above, and against means. Ordinary providence includes means rather than rendering them irrelevant.

“For God, who has appointed every thing his end, he also has ordained the beginning and the means by which we must attain unto the end.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter VI

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The chapter explicitly rejects the claim that providence makes human efforts unprofitable. Its example is Paul’s insistence that the sailors remain aboard the ship despite God’s promise of safety.

06Gospel invitation

THE COMMON CLAIM

Election makes evangelism unnecessary.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“But if this be so, then is all preaching vain.”

John Wesley

Free Grace, Sermon 128, paragraph 10 Text of the 1872 sermon edition, reproduced by the Society of Evangelical Arminians, September 26, 2013

An argument that predestination makes preaching unnecessary

Wesley argues that election fixes salvation with or without preaching. He is drawing a consequence, rather than denying that Calvinists actually preach. Calvin’s response explicitly joins election to proclamation; the historical profiles also document evangelistic practice.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“God wishes that the gospel should be proclaimed to all without exception.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Commentary on 1 Timothy 2:4 Translation: William Pringle (1856)

A gospel to be proclaimed

Calvin expressly teaches the broad proclamation of the gospel. Election does not authorize restricting the invitation to people presumed to be elect.

Read in context. In the surrounding exposition, Calvin interprets “all” in terms of peoples and ranks, rather than a universal decree to save every individual.

Confessional witness · 2 +
“ought to be declared and published to all nations, and to all persons promiscuously and without distinction, to whom God out of his good pleasure sends the gospel.”
Canons of Dort II.5

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The subject is the gospel promise together with the command to repent and believe. “Promiscuously” here means indiscriminately.

“yet we know undoubtedly that it is the will of God that his word should be preached even outwardly.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter I

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The passage affirms inward illumination and drawing by God while insisting on outward preaching. It appeals to Cornelius being sent to Peter and to the opening of Lydia’s heart.

Election & evangelism in history · 7 examples +

From the ancient church to contemporary ministry, these believers joined conviction about God’s sovereign grace with preaching, missionary service, or the sending of preachers. The sources distinguish what each believed from what each did.

These examples answer the claim that belief in election necessarily extinguishes evangelistic zeal. They do not establish that every believer is equally zealous, that all these figures agreed, or that historical examples alone prove the doctrine true. Augustine is an ancient predecessor, not a member of the later Reformed tradition.

  1. Ancient church · 4th–5th centuries

    Augustine of Hippo

    Belief. In his mature teaching, Augustine held that the beginning of faith and perseverance are gifts God gives according to his gracious will. He explicitly argued that preaching predestination must not hinder preaching faith.

    In practice. His surviving sermons put exhortation into practice. Sermon 81 calls hearers to believe in the crucified Christ while explaining faith as the Father’s gift.

    An ancient predecessor, not a Calvinist: Augustine lived long before the Reformation, and his sacramental theology and other teachings differ from later Reformed theology.

    • Belief: Augustine’s own workOn the Gift of Perseverance, chapters 33–36 (hosted as On the Predestination of the Saints, Book II); NPNF English translation
    • Preaching: surviving sermonSermon 81 on the New Testament (Benedictine Sermon 131), sections 2–3; NPNF English translation

  2. Reformation · 16th century

    John Calvin

    Belief. Institutes 3.21.5–7 locates election in God’s eternal counsel, rather than making it depend on foreseen human merit.

    In practice. Alongside his own preaching and writing, Calvin encouraged French Reformed congregations and sent pastors trained in Geneva to serve them.

    This is evidence of active support for spreading Reformed preaching in Europe; it should not be confused with a claim that Calvin personally traveled as an overseas missionary.

    • Belief: Calvin’s own workInstitutes of the Christian Religion 3.21.5–7; Henry Beveridge translation
    • Ministry: historical biographyMusée protestant, Jean Calvin (1509–1564), sections on preaching and France

  3. Evangelical awakening · 18th century

    George Whitefield

    Belief. In his 1740 letter to John Wesley, Whitefield defended election and argued that God appoints preaching as a means of bringing people to salvation.

    In practice. He repeatedly preached outdoors to people beyond ordinary church congregations, carrying his evangelical preaching across Britain and the American colonies.

    Whitefield was a Calvinistic Anglican, not a Presbyterian or Baptist. These examples identify a shared conviction about election rather than agreement on every doctrine.

    • Belief: Whitefield’s own letterLetter to Wesley on Election, section 1, p. 8; letter dated December 24, 1740
    • Ministry: historical biographyBanner of Truth, George Whitefield, biographical account of his field preaching

  4. Missionary movement · 18th–19th centuries

    William Carey

    Belief. Carey belonged to the Particular Baptist tradition. George Smith’s historical biography explicitly identifies him as a Calvinist; this establishes his theological setting without supplying a detailed personal exposition of election.

    In practice. His 1792 Enquiry argued for Christians’ obligation to use means to spread the gospel. He helped establish the Baptist Missionary Society and subsequently served in India.

    The evidence here combines historical identification with missionary advocacy and service. It should not be presented as a verbatim statement of Carey’s precise formulation of election.

    • Belief: historical biographyGeorge Smith, The Life of William Carey, D.D. (1885), chapter I, Carey’s College
    • Missionary advocacy: Carey’s publicationWilliam Carey University’s Carey Center, An Enquiry (1792), overview and original text
    • Ministry: historical recordWilliam Carey University’s Carey Center, Description of the Center and Carey’s work in India

  5. Victorian evangelicalism · 19th century

    Charles Haddon Spurgeon

    Belief. His sermon Election, preached September 2, 1855, openly defends God’s electing choice in salvation.

    In practice. In A Gospel Sermon to Outsiders, preached August 19, 1877, he deliberately addresses the unconverted at an open service and urges them to come to Christ.

    His own sermons supply evidence for both convictions and evangelistic practice, without relying on estimates of audience size or conversion totals.

    • Belief: Spurgeon’s own sermonElection, sermon 41–42, September 2, 1855; historic English text at CCEL
    • Evangelism: Spurgeon’s own sermonA Gospel Sermon to Outsiders, Mark 10:49, August 19, 1877; The Spurgeon Library

  6. Modern evangelicalism · 20th century

    Martyn Lloyd-Jones

    Belief. In Chosen in Him, his 1954 sermon on Ephesians 1:4, Lloyd-Jones defends God’s sovereign choice and expressly denies that election makes evangelism unnecessary.

    In practice. At Westminster Chapel, he devoted Sunday evenings to evangelistic preaching. The MLJ Trust documents the priority he placed on preparing sermons for unbelievers.

    His criticism of some evangelistic campaign methods did not amount to opposition to evangelism; the record includes his regular evangelistic preaching.

    • Belief: sermon archiveChosen in Him, sermon 4007, November 14, 1954; recording and publisher’s summary
    • Ministry: archive’s historical accountGreg Jones, Saints in the morning, sinners at night, MLJ Trust

  7. Contemporary example · 20th–21st centuries

    John Piper

    Belief. His 2000 message Let the Nations Be Glad explicitly affirms unconditional election while arguing that it gives no basis for withholding the gospel from any person or people.

    In practice. In that message he urges churches to raise up missionaries for unreached peoples. His 1991 essay Unreached Peoples develops the case for planting churches where the gospel has not reached.

    The cited evidence demonstrates sustained missionary teaching and mobilization, not a claim that Piper himself served as a career overseas missionary.

    • Belief and missionary exhortation: Piper’s own messageLet the Nations Be Glad, June 15, 2000, Doctrines of Grace and Unconditional Election sections
    • Missionary advocacy: Piper’s own essayUnreached Peoples: The Unique and Primary Goal of Missions, January 1, 1991

Historical summaries and source references checked November 11, 2026. Belief, ministry, and qualifications are editorial summaries, not attributed quotations.

07Gospel invitation

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinists cannot sincerely invite everyone to Christ.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“You represent him as mocking his helpless creatures, by offering what he never intends to give.”

John Wesley

Free Grace, Sermon 128, paragraph 24 Text of the 1872 sermon edition, reproduced by the Society of Evangelical Arminians, September 26, 2013

A challenge to the sincerity of the universal invitation

“Him” is Christ. Wesley questions how invitations to all can be sincere if saving grace is not given to all. He is not alleging that Calvinists never issue invitations. Calvin affirms a genuine general invitation while distinguishing it from the inward call; whether that distinction answers Wesley is the substantive disagreement.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“both to invite all indiscriminately to partake of life, and to cut off every excuse from unbelievers.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Commentary on John 3:16 Translation: William Pringle (1847)

An invitation without distinction

Calvin explains the universal term “whosoever” as an indiscriminate invitation. The promise of life is addressed to everyone who believes.

Read in context. He also distinguishes the universal promise from the particular gift of faith. Whether these fit together is the disputed question.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“As many as are called by the gospel, are unfeignedly called.”
Canons of Dort III/IV.8

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

“Unfeignedly” means sincerely. The rest of the article affirms the serious promise of eternal life to everyone who comes and believes.

08Christian life

THE COMMON CLAIM

Predestination makes prayer pointless.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Our prayers cannot have any effect on the eternal destiny of any individual.”

Ben Henshaw

Examining Inconsistencies in Calvinistic Monergism, Part 1: Intercessory Prayer, paragraph beginning “The Arminian contends” Society of Evangelical Arminians, May 26, 2008; article explicitly credited to Ben Henshaw

A challenge concerning intercessory prayer for salvation

Henshaw is describing what he regards as a consequence of Calvinism, not stating his own view that prayer is ineffective. His preceding sentence limits the point to prayer’s intended effect in another person’s salvation. He also considers the reply that God ordains prayer as a means and disputes its coherence. Calvin’s affirmative account of prayer states the position under discussion.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“prayer digs up those treasures which the Gospel of our Lord discovers to the eye of faith.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 3.20.2 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Prayer as an appointed means

Prayer is, for Calvin, a means of receiving God’s gifts. He devotes an extensive chapter to its necessity, practice, and benefits.

Read in context. He does not describe prayer as supplying God with missing information or overturning his eternal purpose.

Confessional witness · 3 +
“Because it is the chief part of thankfulness which God requires of us”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 45, Q&A 116

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

This answers why prayer is necessary for Christians. The answer also connects receiving God’s grace and Spirit with continual, sincere prayer.

“Prayer, with thanksgiving, being one part of natural worship, is by God required of all men.”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 22.3

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

The paragraph goes on to describe accepted prayer as offered in the Son’s name, by the Spirit’s help, with understanding, humility, faith, love, and perseverance.

“Prayer must be made for the magistracy, for kings, and all that are placed in authority, for ministers of the Church, and for all necessities of churches”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter XXIII

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The sentence continues by requiring unceasing private and public prayer during calamity. The chapter treats prayer as a real duty of the church.

09Christian life

THE COMMON CLAIM

Election and perseverance make holiness unnecessary.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“it directly tends to destroy that holiness which is the end of all the ordinances of God.”

John Wesley

Free Grace, Sermon 128, paragraph 11 Text of the 1872 sermon edition, reproduced by the Society of Evangelical Arminians, September 26, 2013

An alleged tendency, with an explicit qualification

Wesley immediately says that he does not deny the holiness of everyone who holds predestination. His criticism concerns the doctrine’s supposed tendency to weaken motives for holiness. Calvin’s insistence that election leads to holiness answers the broad summary, while the dispute about its motivating force remains.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“We dream not of a faith which is devoid of good works, nor of a justification which can exist without them”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 3.16.1 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Faith that bears fruit

Calvin connects faith and works while distinguishing their roles. He rejects a saving faith that remains barren.

Read in context. The immediate subject is justification by faith. This passage shows the connection of faith and works, rather than answering every objection to perseverance.

Confessional witness · 4 +
“Because Christ, having redeemed and delivered us by his blood, also renews us by his Holy Spirit, after his own image”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 32, Q&A 86

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

This answers why people delivered by grace without merit must still do good works. The answer continues with gratitude, God’s praise, assurance, and witness to neighbors.

“Therefore it is impossible that this holy faith can be unfruitful in man”
Belgic Confession, Article 24

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The article connects living faith with works of love while expressly denying that those works are the basis of justification.

“These good works, done in obedience to God’s commandments, are the fruits and evidences of a true and lively faith”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 16.2

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

Good works are described as evidence and fruit of faith, expressing thankfulness and glorifying God. This does not make works the ground of justification.

“Works do necessarily proceed from faith; but salvation is improperly attributed to them, which is most properly ascribed to grace.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter XVI

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

The chapter rejects contempt for good works while denying that they earn salvation. It presents them as the necessary fruit of living faith.

10Christian life

THE COMMON CLAIM

You can never know whether you are elect.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Calvinism cannot logically bring assurance to those who are consistent within it’s systematic.”

Leighton Flowers

Why Calvinists Cannot have Assurance, paragraph beginning “This final point” Soteriology 101, June 25, 2015; original wording retained

A consistency argument about assurance

Flowers’s argument concerns the possibility of divinely determined self-deception. It is not a survey claiming no Calvinist experiences assurance. Calvin directs believers to Christ rather than speculation about the secret decree; that positive teaching needs to be distinguished from Flowers’s challenge to its coherence.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“Christ, then, is the mirror in which we ought, and in which, without deception, we may contemplate our election.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 3.24.5 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Look to Christ

Calvin directs believers to Christ, rather than speculation about God’s hidden counsel, for assurance of election.

Read in context. This is pastoral counsel to believers. It does not claim that every feeling of confidence is reliable or that believers never experience doubt.

Confessional witness · 3 +
“but also an assured confidence, which the Holy Ghost works by the gospel in my heart”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 7, Q&A 21

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The answer defines true faith as both knowledge and personal confidence in forgiveness and salvation through Christ’s merits.

“may in this life be certainly assured that they are in the state of grace”
Second London Baptist Confession (1689), 18.1

English text as published by Reformed Baptist Seminary; reference gives chapter and paragraph

The subject is those who truly believe in Christ, love him sincerely, and seek to walk in good conscience. Paragraph 18.3 also acknowledges that a true believer may struggle for a long time before enjoying assurance.

“Let Christ, therefore, be our looking-glass, in whom we may behold our predestination.”
Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter X

English version in Philip Schaff, The Creeds of Christendom, Volume III, Appendix; CCEL transcription

“Looking-glass” means mirror. The chapter directs readers to Christ and the preached gospel instead of seeking knowledge of election apart from him.

11Gospel invitation

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinists must say God has no love for mankind.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“God is love only to some men”

John Wesley

Free Grace, Sermon 128, paragraph 20 Text of the 1872 sermon edition, reproduced by the Society of Evangelical Arminians, September 26, 2013

A claim about the scope of divine love

Wesley says this is what predestinarian interpreters infer from election and mercy texts. He does not say they deny every kind of love toward every human being. Calvin’s excerpt affirms divine love toward mankind; it should not be expanded into an assertion that Calvin teaches an identical saving purpose for every individual.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“the Heavenly Father loves the human race, and wishes that they should not perish.”

John Calvin

Commentary on John 3:16 Translation: William Pringle (1847)

The love of God toward mankind

Calvin speaks explicitly of God’s love for the human race when explaining the gift of his Son.

Read in context. The same exposition distinguishes the universal gospel promise from election. Reformed writers differ about the terminology and senses of divine love; this excerpt should not erase those distinctions.

12Christian life

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinism leaves no room for the Holy Spirit.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“it tends to obstruct, if not destroy, this great work of the Holy Ghost”

John Wesley

Free Grace, Sermon 128, paragraph 16 Text of the 1872 sermon edition, reproduced by the Society of Evangelical Arminians, September 26, 2013

A narrower criticism about the Spirit’s witness

“This great work” means the Spirit’s witness and the believer’s assurance discussed in paragraphs 14–16. Wesley does not allege that Calvinists deny every activity of the Holy Spirit. This is a published example related to the broader editorial trope, not an exact equivalent of its sweeping wording. Calvin explicitly makes the Spirit indispensable to receiving Scripture.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

THE COMMON CLAIM

Calvinists follow Calvin instead of Scripture.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

“their theology is not founded on the Bible, but orientated in rationalism”

Friedrich Bente

A related criticism of reason governing scriptural interpretation

Bente makes this claim about both Calvinists and Synergists in a Lutheran defense of the Formula of Concord. “no doctrine is worthy to be believed but that which we find to be grounded in the Scriptures.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

An authority above Calvin

Calvin makes Scripture the test of doctrine, including the doctrine of a respected teacher. His own interpretations remain subject to that test.

Read in context. He is commenting on the Bereans’ examination of Paul’s teaching. Following Calvin as an unquestionable authority would conflict with the principle stated here.

14Christian life

THE COMMON CLAIM

If I preach that, people will live however they want.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“If any one saith, that the man who is justified and how perfect soever, is not bound to observe the commandments of God and of the Church, but only to believe; as if indeed the Gospel were a bare and absolute promise of eternal life, without the condition of observing the commandments ; let him be anathema.”

Council of Trent

Session VI, Decree on Justification, Canon XX (January 13, 1547) J. Waterworth translation (1848), Hanover Historical Texts Project

A historical condemnation related to the objection about obedience

This is Trent’s wording, not the modern preaching slogan above. The council condemns the claim that justified people need only believe and are exempt from commandments. It does not say that every Calvinist lives carelessly. Calvin’s reply denies a faith devoid of good works while distinguishing works from the ground of justification.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“We dream not of a faith which is devoid of good works, nor of a justification which can exist without them”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Institutes 3.16.1 Translation: Henry Beveridge (1845)

Preaching grace and obedience

The objection is that preaching free justification will encourage careless living. Calvin answers that the faith which justifies also bears fruit in obedience. Good works do not earn justification, but they accompany living faith.

Read in context. Calvin is defending justification by faith against the charge that it destroys good works. Trent’s canon below condemns exemption from obedience; Calvin also rejects that exemption. Their disagreement concerns justification and the place of works, not whether Christians should obey God.

15Biblical interpretation

THE COMMON CLAIM

All means all and that’s all that all means.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“all means all and that’s all that all means.”

An unnamed pastor, as recalled by R. Scott Clark

Limited Atonement, discussion of “all” The Heidelblog, R. Scott Clark, April 17, 2009; typographic apostrophe normalized

A common slogan reported by a Reformed respondent

Clark recalls a pastor using this slogan and then argues against it. Clark is reporting the objection, not endorsing it; the pastor is unnamed. The slogan above is therefore presented as a common trope rather than attributed to a named critic or to all non-Calvinists.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“But the present discourse relates to classes of men, and not to individual persons; for his sole object is, to include in this number princes and foreign nations.”

John Calvin

Citation & context +

Commentary on 1 Timothy 2:4 Translation: William Pringle (1856)

Who is included in “all”?

The slogan assumes that “all” must mean every individual without exception. Calvin reads this passage in light of Paul’s call to pray for kings and people in authority: salvation is not confined to one nation or social rank. The question is who “all” includes in this particular passage.

Read in context. This is Calvin’s interpretation of 1 Timothy 2:4, not a rule that “all” can never mean every individual. His interpretation must be assessed against the passage and its context; quoting him establishes his position without settling the exegetical dispute.

OUR APPROACH

A fair hearing.
A faithful reading.

This collection exists to vindicate Calvin and the Reformed tradition where their teaching is misrepresented, by letting their own words be heard.

That purpose calls for care. A statement of belief and a criticism of its logical consequences are different things. Showing that Calvin denied a conclusion does not, by itself, answer a critic’s argument that his theology implies it.
