01Rapture & resurrection

THE COMMON CLAIM

You don’t believe in the rapture.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Well, I think the amillennial belief—and, Tom, it varies depending on who defines it, but there really isn’t any Rapture.”

Dave Hunt

Why Are There So Many Views of the Rapture?, first answer about the amillennial view The Berean Call, Search the Scriptures Daily transcript; webpage dated December 27, 2019 (publication date, not a verified recording date)

A disputed term needs definition

Hunt is answering T. A. McMahon about differing rapture views. He identifies the subject as amillennial belief and qualifies it by acknowledging varying definitions. The transcript’s later publication date should not be read as the date Hunt recorded it.

IN THEIR OWN WORDS

“yes, there is a "catching away" of believers”

OPC Q&A (unsigned answer)

Citation & context +

What is the OPC’s view on the rapture?, paragraph beginning “In the sense” Source: Orthodox Presbyterian Church Q&A, April 3, 2011

The gathering at Christ’s coming

This answer affirms the catching up described in 1 Thessalonians 4:16–17. It understands the gathering and resurrection as part of Christ’s visible, personal, glorious return. The difference concerns the timing and sequence of the event, rather than whether believers will be gathered to Christ.

Read in context. Hunt’s conversation uses “rapture” within a discussion of competing end-times schemes and notes variation in definitions. If he means a separate pretribulation event, the disagreement is real. But the phrase must not be taken to mean that amillennialists deny the catching up in Paul’s passage. The OPC answer is a published explanation, not itself a constitutional confession, and it does not speak for every non-dispensational Christian.

Scripture to examine

  • 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 · resurrection and catching up
  • 1 Corinthians 15:50–54 · the dead raised and the living changed
  • 2 Thessalonians 2:1–2 · Christ’s coming and our gathering
More voices · 2 +
“the resurrection of believers from the state of life or death to glorification at the return of our Lord.”

Kim Riddlebarger · A Case for Amillennialism, expanded edition (2013), chapter 1, “The Rapture,” p. 30 Baker Books, 2013; publisher’s preview hosted by Christian Study Library

The two passages under discussion are 1 Corinthians 15:50–55 and 1 Thessalonians 4:13–5:11. Riddlebarger places the gathering at Christ’s bodily return and distinguishes this from a separate pretribulation rapture. The excerpt describes his account of the historic Protestant reading; it is not a claim that all Protestants agree.

ANCIENT WITNESS · FOURTH/FIFTH CENTURY

“And as He descends, we go forth to meet Him”

John Chrysostom · Homily 8 on First Thessalonians, exposition of 1 Thessalonians 4:16–18, paragraph beginning “For He received” Early Christian author (c. 349–407); NPNF, First Series, vol. 13 (1889), English text as revised and published by New Advent

Chrysostom expressly describes believers being caught up to meet the descending Lord and joins the meeting to the resurrection. He compares it to going out to greet an arriving king. This supports a real gathering at Christ’s coming, without making him a participant in the modern debate over a pretribulation rapture.

Confessional witness · 2 +
“At the last day, such as are found alive shall not die, but be changed:”
Westminster Confession of Faith 32.2

Historic English text as published by the Orthodox Presbyterian Church

The same paragraph affirms bodily resurrection of all the dead. It supports a real transformation of living believers, without using the modern label “rapture.”

“this my body, being raised by the power of Christ, shall be reunited with my soul,”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 22, Q. 57

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The catechism explicitly confesses bodily resurrection. Its affirmation alone does not decide every question about tribulation or millennial chronology.

The creator’s position +

PERSONAL STATEMENT · SITE CREATOR

“those who are alive will be caught up to meet the Lord in the air.”

A Pastoral Confession of Faith, XIII, The Resurrection and Gathering of His People

The site creator explicitly affirms the gathering with the resurrection at Christ’s one glorious appearing. His addendum permits liberty among millennial views within its stated doctrinal boundaries. This personal confession guides the collection’s scope; it is not a historic Reformed standard.

02Israel & the church

THE COMMON CLAIM

You believe in replacement theology.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“We then receive all the promises; the Jews get nothing but the curses”

John MacArthur

Why Every Calvinist Should Be a Premillennialist, Part 2, discussion defining replacement theology Grace to You sermon transcript, April 1, 2007; sermon 90-335

Description of a position and an interpretive disagreement

MacArthur is describing what he rejects, not endorsing it. He argues that Israel’s unbelief cannot cancel God’s promises and connects that concern to a future national kingdom. The response distinguishes a Reformed writer’s own account from that description; it does not settle the disputed fulfillment of each promise.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“the Reformed view does not regard the gospel as “replacing” the older covenant economy with Israel but “fulfilling” it.”

Cornelis Venema

Citation & context +

The Church and Israel: The Issue, “Extreme Replacement Theology” Source: Ligonier Ministries / Tabletalk, October 1, 2012

Fulfillment in Christ, one people in him

Venema distinguishes fulfillment through Israel’s Messiah from the idea that God has discarded the Jewish people for a Gentile church. His account gathers Jewish and Gentile believers into one people through faith in Christ. The promises are received in him, rather than taken away from Jews simply because they are Jews.

Read in context. “Replacement theology” and “supersessionism” are used with different definitions. MacArthur also argues that specific promises require a future national fulfillment; saying “fulfillment” does not by itself answer that argument. Reformed writers differ about Romans 11 and a future conversion of ethnic Israel. The site creator’s confession affirms Israel’s descendants remain beloved, but it should not be expanded into a claim that he endorses every proposal about national restoration, land, or a future millennium.

Scripture to examine

  • Romans 11:17–29 · natural branches, grafting, and the warning against boasting
  • Ephesians 2:11–22 · Jew and Gentile reconciled in one body
  • Galatians 3:16, 28–29 · Christ, Abraham’s offspring, and the heirs
More voices · 2 +
“To be in Christ is to be a child of Abraham.”

Kim Riddlebarger · Jesus Christ — The Israel of God, concluding clarification after the Robert Strimple quotation The Riddleblog, January 13, 2022

Riddlebarger locates participation in Abraham’s promises in union with Christ, whom he identifies as the true Israel. He also rejects a reconstituted national Israel after Christ’s return as the fulfillment he is discussing. His view should not be represented as either denying Jews salvation or affirming the distinct national program he explicitly disputes.

ANCIENT WITNESS · FOURTH/FIFTH CENTURY

“Behold, they are exalted to one and the same dignity, the two have become one,”

John Chrysostom · Homily 5 on Ephesians, exposition of Ephesians 2:13, paragraph beginning “Behold” Early Christian author (c. 349–407); Gross Alexander translation, NPNF, First Series, vol. 13 (1889), revised for New Advent

The two are Jewish and Gentile believers, united in Christ rather than one group enjoying a superior saving status. Later in the homily he describes both being made new in Christ. This is ancient witness to their unity, not proof that Chrysostom rejected every view now called replacement theology or endorsed every claim about Israel’s national future.

Confessional witness · 2 +
“out of the whole human race, a church chosen to everlasting life, agreeing in true faith;”
Heidelberg Catechism, Lord’s Day 21, Q. 54

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The answer describes Christ gathering his church throughout history. It affirms a worldwide people of faith without resolving every promise about ethnic Israel.

“This Church has been from the beginning of the world, and will be to the end thereof;”
Belgic Confession, Article 27

Historic English text as published by the Free Reformed Churches of North America

The confession describes continuity of Christ’s people across the ages. This passage does not specify a future national or political role for Israel.

The creator’s position +

PERSONAL STATEMENT · SITE CREATOR

“remain beloved for the sake of the promises”

A Pastoral Confession of Faith, IX, The Church

The subject is Israel’s physical descendants. The same section affirms that Jewish and Gentile believers form one people in Christ. This personal statement does not identify ethnic descent alone with saving membership in Christ or define every future national expectation.

03Christ’s kingdom

THE COMMON CLAIM

You don’t believe in a literal millennium.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“there will be no thousand-year reign of Christ on the earth.”

John MacArthur

Why Every Calvinist Should Be a Premillennialist, Part 1, paragraph beginning “But, for certain” Grace to You, sermon 90-334

A real disagreement about the millennium

MacArthur is describing the amillennial position he rejects. He immediately distinguishes it from the future earthly millennium he affirms and elsewhere in this discussion acknowledges spiritual and heavenly readings. The objection is about what Revelation 20 promises, rather than whether Christ has any kingdom.

ANCIENT WITNESS · IN HIS OWN WORDS

“Therefore the Church even now is the kingdom of Christ, and the kingdom of heaven. Accordingly, even now His saints reign with Him, though otherwise than as they shall reign hereafter;”

Augustine of Hippo

Citation & context +

The City of God, Book XX, chapter 9, paragraph beginning “We must understand” Source: Marcus Dods translation, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, First Series, vol. 2, edited by Philip Schaff (1887); CCEL transcription

A present reign, awaiting its fullness

Augustine affirms a real kingdom and a real reign with Christ now, while distinguishing that present reign from its future fullness. He opens this chapter by identifying the saints’ reign during the thousand years with the time of Christ’s first coming; the edition’s note clarifies the interval between his first and second comings. This is an interpretation of what Revelation 20 describes, rather than a denial that its promised reign exists.

Read in context. Augustine is an ancient Christian witness, not a sixteenth-century Reformer or a spokesman for every modern amillennial position. His present reign includes both living believers and the faithful departed; the chapter also contains sacramental teaching not adopted wholesale here. Calvin’s supporting passage likewise places the thousand years in the church’s life in this world. Neither writer affirms an exact thousand-year earthly kingdom after Christ returns. That disagreement remains explicit; the point is that they affirm the reality of Christ’s reign while locating the passage’s fulfillment differently. The creator’s confession allows liberty among millennial views within its stated boundaries.

Scripture to examine

  • Revelation 20:1-6
  • Colossians 1:13
  • 1 Corinthians 15:25-26
More voices · 1 +
“since the thousand years there mentioned refer not to the eternal blessedness of the Church, but only to the various troubles which await the Church militant in this world.”

John Calvin · Institutes of the Christian Religion III.25.5, discussion of Revelation 20:4 Henry Beveridge translation (1845); CCEL transcription

“Church militant” means the church in its present earthly struggle. Calvin places this passage’s thousand years here, rather than in the final blessedness after resurrection. His surrounding criticism also targets limiting the saints’ future happiness to one thousand years. Modern premillennialists generally affirm everlasting blessedness too, so that part of his criticism should not be assigned to them without qualification. The excerpt is Calvin’s text, distinct from the later editor’s notes.

Confessional witness · 2 +
“is the kingdom of the Lord Jesus Christ,”
Westminster Confession of Faith 25.2

Historic English text as published by the linked church body

The subject is the visible church. The paragraph also includes Presbyterian teaching about believers’ children; its whole ecclesiology should not be attributed to every Reformed Baptist.

“preserve and increase thy church; destroy the works of the devil,”
Heidelberg Catechism, Q. 123

Historic English text as published by the linked church body

The prayer seeks the growth of the church now and the full perfection of God’s kingdom. It does not prescribe a millennial chronology.

04Reading prophecy

THE COMMON CLAIM

You don’t take the Bible literally.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Spiritualizing a passage is dangerous because it moves the basis for accurate interpretation from Scripture to the mind of the reader.”

GotQuestions.org

What is premillennialism?, discussion of spiritualizing Published text at the linked primary source

An interpretive disagreement

The article allows figures of speech where context warrants them. Its objection is to importing meanings without textual warrant, not to all symbolic language.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“to interpret the Bible literally is to interpret the Bible the way it was written.”

R. C. Sproul

Citation & context +

How to Interpret the Bible Literally, transcript paragraph explaining sensus literalis Source: Ultimately with R. C. Sproul, Ligonier Ministries, episode published July 2, 2025; publication date of this excerpt, not a verified recording date

Literal reading and literary form

Sproul directly affirms literal interpretation and defines it by the way a text was written. He distinguishes historical narrative, poetry, parables, and other literary forms. He also explicitly rejects turning historical miracle accounts into moral symbolism. On that definition, recognizing a symbol is part of taking the text seriously, not permission to explain away the events it records.

Read in context. This passage states a Reformed principle of interpretation; it is not itself an exposition of Revelation 20. Premillennial interpreters also recognize figures of speech, as the critic’s full article makes clear. Their disagreement with amillennialists concerns which passages are symbolic and how to identify their fulfillment. Sproul’s principle corrects a blanket accusation, but each disputed interpretation still needs to be defended from the actual passage. The supporting excerpt applies Scripture-interpreting-Scripture to Revelation specifically.

Scripture to examine

  • Revelation 1:20 · the vision explains its own symbols
  • Psalm 91:4 · wings and refuge in poetic language
  • Luke 1:1–4 · historical testimony and careful narration
More voices · 2 +
“we must read the Book of Revelation through the rest of the Bible.”

Kim Riddlebarger · A Present or Future Millennium, paragraph beginning “Following this method” Modern Reformation, author’s essay in the publisher’s online archive

Riddlebarger argues that clearer biblical teaching should govern the reading of difficult apocalyptic passages. This identifies his proposed interpretive rule; it does not establish that every application of that rule is correct or that premillennialists reject comparing Scripture with Scripture.

ANCIENT WITNESS · FOURTH/FIFTH CENTURY

“we must also pay heed to that which tells us not to take a literal form of speech as if it were figurative.”

Augustine of Hippo · On Christian Doctrine III.10.14, opening sentence Early Christian author (354–430); James Shaw translation, NPNF, First Series, vol. 2 (1887), revised for New Advent

Augustine gives this rule alongside the complementary warning against treating metaphorical language as literal. The excerpt answers the idea that recognizing figures gives unlimited permission to allegorize. His particular rules and applications still require evaluation; this passage is not itself an interpretation of the millennium.

Confessional witness · 1 +
“The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself:”
Westminster Confession of Faith 1.9

Historic English text as published by the linked church body

The whole paragraph requires comparison with clearer passages. This is a stated rule to assess, rather than proof that every proposed interpretation follows it correctly.

05Christian hope

THE COMMON CLAIM

Your view is pessimistic and defeatist.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Historically amillennialism has tended to be pessimistic in terms of the question of widespread, long-lasting cultural success”

Kenneth L. Gentry, Jr.

Is Amillennialism Pessimistic?, paragraph beginning “Historically” Postmillennial Worldview, July 13, 2021

A broad description that needs qualification

Gentry expressly acknowledges that evangelical amillennialists expect Christ’s final victory. His criticism concerns sustained cultural success before the Second Coming.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“The kingdom of Jesus Christ will grow and thrive all the while persecution and political conditions appear to be getting worse”

Kim Riddlebarger

Citation & context +

The Binding of Satan, paragraph discussing nations that persecute Christians Source: The Riddleblog, June 13, 2022

Hope through suffering

Riddlebarger expects the gospel to accomplish its purpose and Christ’s kingdom to grow, including under persecution. His stated hope concerns the church’s mission in the present age, not only victory after history has ended. He explicitly distinguishes that growth from a guarantee of corresponding cultural, economic, or political progress.

Read in context. Gentry acknowledges the final victory affirmed by other evangelical millennial views. His criticism concerns widespread, lasting cultural success before Christ’s return. Riddlebarger’s expectation of gospel advance answers a broad charge of defeatism but does not affirm Gentry’s particular expectation of cultural transformation. Their historical disagreement is real. Gordon’s supporting statement similarly identifies growth of the church and the overthrow of the devil’s works as grounds for hope.

Scripture to examine

  • Matthew 16:18
  • John 16:33
  • Revelation 7:9-17
More voices · 2 +
“That is a thoroughly optimistic view of the kingdom of God.”

Chris Gordon · Amillennialism Is Not Pessimistic, concluding sentence The Heidelblog, January 20, 2024

“That” refers to prayer for God to rule by his Word and Spirit, preserve and increase the church, and destroy the devil’s works until Christ’s return. Gordon explicitly calls that expectation optimistic; it does not include the cultural dominance Gentry expects.

ANCIENT WITNESS · FOURTH/FIFTH CENTURY

“the greater the intensity of our persecutions, the greater should be the increase of your good hope;”

John Chrysostom · Homily 2 on Second Corinthians, exposition of 2 Corinthians 1:6, paragraph beginning “Do you see again” Early Christian author (c. 349–407); Talbot W. Chambers translation, NPNF, First Series, vol. 12 (1889), revised for New Advent

Chrysostom is explaining Paul’s consolation to suffering Christians: persecution gives grounds for strengthened hope rather than despair. This supplies an ancient foundation for hope through suffering. It does not establish that Chrysostom would adopt a particular modern forecast about the church’s cultural influence.

Heidelberg Catechism, Q. 123

06Israel’s future

THE COMMON CLAIM

You think God is finished with the Jews.

Editorial summary · see the published criticism below

Published criticism & context +

“Israel has been permanently set aside”

John MacArthur

Why Every Calvinist Should Be a Premillennialist, Part 1, discussion of Israel’s forfeited privileges Grace to You, sermon 90-334

A broad description that needs qualification

MacArthur attributes this conclusion to amillennialists and postmillennialists in a discussion of national covenant promises. He is not saying they forbid individual Jewish conversions.

IN HIS OWN WORDS

“The future restoration of the nation of Israel will involve their re-grafting into the olive tree, the one people of God.”

Keith Mathison

Citation & context +

The Church and Israel in the New Testament, conclusion Source: Ligonier Ministries / Tabletalk, October 1, 2012

Praying for Jewish people to come to Christ

Mathison explicitly expects a future restoration of ethnic Israel through faith in Jesus Christ. He understands that restoration as being grafted into the one people of God, rather than a separate way of salvation. This is a direct example of a Reformed writer who rejects the idea that God is finished with the Jews.

Read in context. Mathison’s expectation is not shared by all Reformed interpreters: his article expressly discusses differing readings of “all Israel” in Romans 11. His position should not be assigned to Calvin, to every amillennialist, or to the site creator merely because they share some other convictions. MacArthur’s specific expectation of a future national kingdom is a further question; conversion and re-grafting do not by themselves establish every territorial or political claim. The creator’s linked confession affirms Israel’s descendants remain beloved without defining all these expectations.

Scripture to examine

  • Romans 11:1-2
  • Romans 11:23-29
  • Romans 10:1

OUR APPROACH

A fair hearing.
A faithful reading.

This collection clarifies what writers affirm about Christ’s return and his people before weighing their disagreements. Six entries examine familiar accusations about the rapture, replacement theology, Christ’s kingdom, spiritualizing Scripture, defeatism, and whether God is finished with the Jews. The common claims are editorial summaries; the attributed quotations preserve what each critic actually said.

The scope is informed by the creator’s personal confession, which affirms Christ’s bodily return, resurrection, judgment, and one people in him, while allowing liberty among millennial views within its stated boundaries. The collection is therefore broader than a defense of one millennial label. Every entry includes an early Christian witness, with the point of agreement and its limits identified; ancient testimony is not treated as unanimity or as proof of an author’s agreement with an entire modern system.

Each entry leads with an editorial summary. The exact published criticism, attribution, citation, and context are preserved in “Published criticism & context,” closed by default. Context notes retain qualifications and distinguish a broad description of someone’s beliefs from an argument about their consequences. Dispensational writers differ too; a quotation does not stand for every premillennial or dispensational Christian.

“Rapture” can mean the catching up itself or a particular sequence of events. “Replacement theology” also has several definitions. Stating a position accurately does not settle the timing of the gathering, the interpretation of Romans 11, or the fulfillment of every promise to Israel. Jewish and Gentile identity, saving membership in Christ, and modern political claims should not be treated as interchangeable questions.

Modern excerpts are brief and should be read in their full sources. The personal confession is identified separately from historical confessional witness; neither the OPC Q&A nor any individual writer is made to speak for all Reformed Christians.
